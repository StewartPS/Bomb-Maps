    How this layer was built

    2,397 points come from an original scan of the census map, georeferenced against three checkable landmarks. A further 646 come from a second overlay source, after 308 that fell within 40m of a census point were removed as probable duplicates.

    Only the overlay source recorded bomb types at all, which is why most of the layer is one colour: 566 points are marked high explosive and 48 incendiary, and the remaining 2,429 — the 2,397 census dots, printed with no type, plus 32 overlay pins whose type could not be read — are shown together as “type not recorded”. Those two were separate colours until people reasonably asked what the difference was; it is a difference of source, not of evidence.

    The Dockyard basins show almost no dots on the census map — most likely a wartime security omission on the original document rather than a gap here.

    Does the total look right? The Home Office bombing statistics record 2,820 weapons on Plymouth — 2,584 high explosive, 190 phosphorus, 27 parachute mines, 18 fire pots and one oil bomb — across 9,525 acres. This layer plots 3,043 points across a footprint of about 9,240 acres. Two independent measures, count and area, both land within single figures of the official record, which is reassuring for a layer built by georeferencing a scanned map. The small excess is most likely near‑duplicates between the two sources that survived the 40m merge.

    It is not everything that fell. Incendiaries were dropped in their thousands — Plymouth City Council puts the March–April 1941 raids alone at “over 6,000 general purpose bombs and 205,000 incendiary bombs” — and were never plotted individually by anyone, because nobody could. What you are looking at is roughly where the high explosive fell, not the full weight of the raids. Note too that the council’s 6,000 for seven nights and the Home Office’s 2,584 for the whole war cannot both be counting the same thing; both are recorded here rather than one being chosen.

    No point carries an individual date, so each is assigned a modelled year (1940–44) weighted by documented raid intensity. That drives the timeline; it is not a claim about any single point.

    Wider archive context and the Town Clerk’s wartime bomb book are held by The Box, Plymouth. The bomb book covers 59 raids from 6 July 1940 to 30 April 1944 but excludes Devonport Dockyard, Plympton and Plymstock, which is a second reason this layer thins out over the Dockyard. Official totals above from the Home Office bombing statistics as published in 1st Line Defence’s Plymouth UXO guide; raid figures from Plymouth City Council and The Box.

    Transparency

    Sources and next steps

    Headline figures come from Plymouth City Council's public record. Archive context comes from The Box, Plymouth (which now holds the former Plymouth & West Devon Record Office collections). Recent incident details are drawn from council updates and contemporary news coverage. Historic area points remain approximate and should be replaced with archive-backed coordinates before publication.

    On accuracy: positions here are indicative rather than survey-grade. Most confirmed records name a street, not an address, so the point is placed along that street and can sit some way from the specific building involved. Each pin is drawn inside a translucent circle showing that margin — from about 30m for a named building up to 500m for a record that only describes an area — and the same figure is repeated as “Position accuracy” in every record's detail panel. The potential-sites layer is digitised from printed bomb census mapping, where a single dot covers roughly 35–45m of ground. Treat every point as “somewhere in this area”. Nothing on this site should be used to assess unexploded-ordnance risk at a specific address — if you have a genuine concern, contact your local council or the police.
